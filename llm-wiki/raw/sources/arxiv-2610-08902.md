# What the paper says

- Problem: agent evaluations "largely measure what an agent can do at a fixed point in time rather than how effectively it learns." The paper asks whether future performance improves and generalizes beyond the interactions that enabled learning, how efficiently new capabilities are acquired, and where self-improvement breaks down.[^p1]
- Protocol (sec 2.1): the model's weights stay fixed. An agent at checkpoint t is a fixed model M and an artifact inventory I_t of "executable Python tools, natural-language strategies or skills, and textual memory." Each acting episode begins in a fresh context, so "conversational history and temporary files do not persist." After training episodes, the same model reflects on the evidence and proposes an update to the inventory. Updates must pass structural and executable checks before they become persistent; rejected attempts leave the inventory unchanged and still count toward cost. The chain of inventories is an "agent lineage".[^p3]
- Splits (sec 2.1): training interactions (their trajectories reach reflection), held-out in-distribution (ID) interactions, and held-out out-of-distribution (OOD) interactions with stronger game opponents. "Neither held-out trajectories nor their scores are exposed to reflection."[^p4]
- Learning cost (sec 2.1): model calls to interact with the environment and to revise artifacts, including rejected reflection attempts. Token usage is expressed in dollars "as the closest available proxy". "This measures learning cost, not physical computation."[^p4]
- Plasticity (sec 2.2): "held-out performance gain per unit learning cost", reported in score points per $1,000 of learning cost; it can be negative and depends on the horizon. The headline measure is plasticity to estimated saturation, P_sat: held-out ID scores are fitted with S(C) = S0 + A C^h / (C^h + C50^h) by binomial maximum likelihood; C* is the cost of the first observed checkpoint at which the fit reaches 90% of its rise; P_sat is the fitted rise at C* divided by C*. If the fitted 90% rise is below 10 percentage points, P_sat is set to 0. If no observed checkpoint reaches the threshold, the fitted 90% point is extrapolated up to three times the final observed cost; beyond that, plasticity to date is reported instead. Intervals use a parametric bootstrap.[^p4][^p5]
- Diagnosing the loop (sec 2.3): for each identified decision failure, was a covering artifact absent (F_absent), present but unused (F_missed), or used with the failure remaining (F_used)? Decision-level reuse is measured among "relevant decisions", moves where at least one saved artifact is relevant (about 98% of chess moves after checkpoint 0). Labels combine semantic judgments by Gemini 3.1 Pro with execution evidence (file reads, Python invocations). "These diagnostics are observational: artifact use does not establish causal effectiveness."[^p5]
- Setup (sec 3.1, Table 2): each actor runs in an isolated Python container with a restricted board API. Hard chess: Stockfish 16 at 20,000 nodes per move; each checkpoint plays 16 training, 12 held-out ID and 12 held-out OOD games; 20 update rounds. Scores are 100(W + 0.5D)/N, and an unfinished game counts as a loss. Chess has eight models; Go (5 x 5) and Hex (6 x 6) use a subset; NetHack uses models through Claude Code and Codex, six lineages over checkpoints 0 to 40, ten fresh games per checkpoint (2,460 games). Between checkpoints the model may edit CLAUDE.md or AGENTS.md, skills, Python tools, strategies and memory. Actor and reflection calls use the same model.[^p6][^p19]
- Persistent improvement varies (sec 3.2, Figure 3): in Hard chess, Claude Fable 5 and Claude Opus 5 rise from 37.5% and 25.0% at checkpoint 0 to 73.3% and 66.4% held-out ID, averaged over checkpoints 16 to 20. GPT-5.6 Sol starts near 0% and reaches 36.9%. Claude Opus 4.8 improves mainly in Easy chess (0% to 19.0%). "Several other models remain close to their initial performance, and a few decline." OOD gains are generally smaller or less consistent. "The common learning interface therefore permits improvement but does not ensure it."[^p6][^p7]
- Other games (sec 3.3, Figure 5): in Go, Claude Fable 5 rises from 20% to 80% held-out ID and from 0% to 50% OOD; in Hex, GPT-5.6 Sol from 40% to 77.5% ID and from 28.1% to 78.1% OOD. Claude Opus 4.8 loses ground in Go but gains in Hex; Claude Opus 5 gains in Go until a tool-making error at the final checkpoint. In NetHack, Claude Opus 5.5's mean score grows from about 2k to about 6k, saturating at checkpoint 11 with P_sat = 61.75 normalized score points per $1,000; GPT-5.6 Luna and GPT-5.6 Sol decline. Finding 1: persistent self-improvement is model- and task-dependent.[^p7]
- Acquisition efficiency (sec 3.4, Figure 1): on the combined chess, Go and Hex curve, "GPT-5.6 Sol has the highest estimated P_sat at 298 percentage points per $1,000 of mean per-game learning cost, followed by Claude Opus 5 at 233, Claude Fable 5 at 57, and Claude Opus 4.8 at 14". GPT-5.6 Luna shows no reliable rise and is assigned 0. GPT-5.5's saturation cannot be established. Claude Fable 5 reaches the highest fitted performance level. "We therefore interpret plasticity as a protocol-dependent measure, not a universal model ranking." Finding 2: endpoint capability and acquisition efficiency answer different questions.[^p7][^p8]
- Where the loop breaks (sec 3.5, Figures 4, 6, 7): pooled over Hard and Easy chess, GPT-5.6 Luna takes about 98% of relevant decisions without artifact use, Gemini 3.1 Pro about 87%, Claude Opus 4.6 about 51% and GPT-5.5 about 28%, against 2 to 6% for Claude Fable 5, Claude Opus 5, Claude Opus 4.8 and GPT-5.6 Sol. Across 19 model-game cells, held-out ID reuse correlates with the gain from checkpoint 0 to checkpoints 16 to 20. The four strong reusers use artifacts at 94 to 98% of relevant chess decisions and still fail: about 0.16 versus 0.38 identified failures per relevant decision, with 83 to 99% of their remaining failures happening while a relevant artifact is in use. Reuse can rise before performance peaks, and GPT-5.5 uses artifacts at 96 to 100% of relevant decisions at checkpoints 2 to 8 but at 32 to 50% from checkpoint 9 on, although its artifacts did not change. Finding 3: reuse is associated with improvement but is not a proxy for capability.[^p8][^p9]
- Concrete examples (p. 9, Figure 8): one Claude Fable 5 lineage writes its own chess engine without outside libraries, then patches it after specific mistakes (a search for moves out of check, repeated-position handling, more endgame search time) and writes a helper that plays several moves per call under a fixed search budget. In NetHack, Claude Opus 5.5 dies at checkpoint 1 while praying through raw keystrokes ("Killed by a dust vortex, while praying."), adds a rule to CLAUDE.md to pray only through its own controller, and at checkpoint 10 the prayer heals it. "Writing down an instruction is not the same as following it. In one GPT-5.5 run, the reflection step keeps telling the agent to load its saved move-picking program right away, yet the agent still spends its first steps listing and reading files."[^p9][^p10]
- Discussion and limits (sec 4, p. 10 to 11): plasticity "is a property of an agent under a particular learning protocol rather than an intrinsic scalar property of the underlying model"; the setup does not separate the value of environment feedback from the extra computation used to analyze experience and revise artifacts; separately trained lineages "do not establish transfer of learned artifacts or improvement strategies across tasks"; evaluation sets are finite; "plasticity estimates depend on score headroom, the learning horizon, saturation-fit assumptions, and provider pricing." The authors suggest controlled interventions (artifact removal, replacement, forced invocation) and budget-matched reflection without feedback as future work, and suggest post-training could target plasticity directly.[^p10][^p11]
