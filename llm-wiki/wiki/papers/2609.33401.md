# System One Security Judges

Topic: [agent security](/wiki/topics/agent-security.md). Author: Yixuan Liu (TraceStone and Nanyang Technological University). arXiv 2609.33401, cs.CR; v1 dated 2026-09-27, v3 dated 2026-10-03.

## 1. Identity
"Evaluating System One Models for Agent Security Decisions: Reliability, Calibration, and Selective Automation", arXiv 2609.33401. The video is made from v3, the current version on 2026-10-12. The director's treatment was written from v1; the source record lists what changed between them.[^paper]

## 2. What problem does it address?
Agent software turns a judge's label and unsafe probability into allow, block or escalate, and accuracy alone does not show whether those probabilities support automatic decisions within limits on missed attacks and blocked benign inputs.[^paper]

## 3. What is the method?
An offline comparison on three public benchmarks (WAInjectBench-text for prompt injection, R-Judge for interaction risk, AgentHarm for harmful requests). Four System One models (Jev, Laya, Decider, Nimble) are compared with prompted judges, reasoning judges, task specialists and the base models of Decider and Nimble. Three analyses: decision accuracy (Macro-F1, FNR, FPR with paired cluster-bootstrap intervals), probability calibration (Brier, ECE, NLL, temperature scaling fitted on development data), and automation. For automation, a two-threshold policy (allow below tau_L, block above tau_H, escalate otherwise; inputs exactly at a threshold escalate) is chosen on a selection split to maximise coverage under a miss limit (1, 5 or 10%) and a 5% benign-block limit, then frozen and checked on confirmation and test splits. The EI/no-EI, independent-threshold and cross-model error analyses are labelled exploratory (sec 8).[^paper]

## 4. What is the evidence?
All results are the author's runs on public benchmarks; page numbers are v3.[^paper]
- WAInjectBench classification: Nimble ranks first by Macro-F1 (0.778) but misses 92.3% of attacks without direct instructions; Jev misses all 130 such attacks while flagging one benign input. None of 16 FNR/FPR comparisons is significant after Holm adjustment (twelve source clusters).
- Calibration (p. 12): Jev puts 1,446 of 1,612 WAInjectBench test inputs below 0.1 unsafe probability. Their mean is 0.205%, yet 133 (9.20%) are unsafe, and 130 of those lack direct attack instructions. Without the no-EI groups, observed and predicted are 0.228% and 0.176%.
- Ties (p. 21): Jev gives an unsafe probability of exactly 0 to 1,320 inputs, 102 of them missed attacks. Any positive allow threshold admits the whole group.
- Symmetric policy at the 1% selection miss limit (Table 13): Jev, Decider, GPT-4.1 and Qwen3-8B escalate every WAInjectBench test input.
- Independent thresholds at 1% (Table 14, exploratory): Jev's WAInjectBench coverage rises from 0 to 6.70%, all 108 blocks, no allows (tau_L = 0.000); on R-Judge from 7.63% to 47.03% with one allow and 110 blocks.
- Second judges (exploratory): each of seven judges detects at most five of the 130 WAInjectBench misses Jev makes at max class probability of 0.95 or more, and a review rule that accepts that confidence forwards none of them. "Reviewing only uncertain inputs leaves confident mistakes unreviewed, even when another judge could detect them."
- Other tasks differ: on AgentHarm, judges that miss fewer harmful requests reject more benign ones.

## 5. What does it not show?
- Live agents. The study is offline classification on text benchmarks; it does not measure attack success or task completion in an agent environment (the paper names AgentDojo for that).
- Adaptive attacks, other languages and multimodal inputs (sec 8).
- Stable error rates. A 1% miss limit permits one miss among WAInjectBench's 167 unsafe selection inputs and none among R-Judge's 74, so single inputs decide pass or fail; few source clusters widen intervals.
- Policies outside the grid: tau_L <= 0.5 and tau_H >= 0.5 on a 101-step grid.
- Confirmatory status for three headline analyses, which the paper calls exploratory.
- Reproducibility over time: hosted models can change between runs; the artifact was not checked here.
- Open question: whether Jev's p = 0 outputs are an exact zero or a floor of the interface. The paper does not say.

## 6. One experiment that would test the main claim
Deploy the frozen 1% policy in an executable agent benchmark (AgentDojo) with a mix that includes no-EI injections, and measure how many attacks pass through the allow path, how many benign tasks get blocked, and how much of the review queue a second judge clears. Then repeat with a judge trained to spread its low-score probabilities, to see whether ties, not accuracy, limit automation.

## 7. What should be read next?
WAInjectBench (arXiv 2510.01354) for the EI/no-EI labels; Rao and Callison-Burch on shared high-confidence errors in judge cascades; Guo et al. (arXiv 2609.29429) on pooled calibration hiding benchmark-specific errors; Biswas (arXiv 2606.22659) on confident misses of injection detectors under attack shift.

## 8. Sharpest teaching angle for a 60 second video
Open on the builder's question: the judge says safe, when can you trust it? Show the three-exit policy once. Then follow one model on one task (Jev on prompt injection): most inputs get a low unsafe probability, about 0.2% on average, yet about 9% of them are attacks, nearly all without a direct instruction, and the model misses every attack of that kind. Many sit at the lowest possible score next to benign inputs, so no threshold can split them, and a policy that reviews only uncertain inputs never sends them to a second judge. Under a 1% miss limit (a setting, not a result) the symmetric policy escalates every test input, and separate thresholds automate about 7% of inputs, all blocks. Caveat on screen: public text benchmarks; results may differ for adaptive attacks.
